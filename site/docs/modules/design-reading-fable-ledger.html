What Can an Unfinished Life Tell Us?

1. The open line

On the morning the king threatened to close the infirmary, the archivist drew a line beside her mother’s name.

She drew it as far as the day’s date and stopped. Beyond the nib lay a clean stretch of paper. She put a brass weight on the page to keep it from curling, then went to see her mother.

The infirmary was the warmest chamber in the kingdom. Its walls smelled of damp earth and boiled roots. Cups of water stood in niches for patients who could no longer walk to the cistern. Her mother sat beneath a blanket while an attendant eased a sore joint.

“You look as if you’ve swallowed a pin,” her mother said.

“The king wants an answer by the next bell.”

“Give him two. He likes having a choice.”

“He wants to know whether his subjects become more likely to die as they grow older. If they don’t, he says we have no need for an infirmary.”

Her mother looked at the attendant’s hands. “Ask him to try getting out of my chair.”

The king’s notice had already been nailed beside the door. At the bottom was a drawing of a lock, so even those who could not read would understand.

2. The customary book

The kingdom lay sheltered deep underground. Its passages were counted, its households visited, its births entered in the great book. For the king’s inquiry, the archivist began each line at six months, the customary age for adult work.

In the council chamber, the Chancellor ran a claw down a page. “Most have no bell. Strike them out.”

“My mother’s line has no bell,” the archivist said. “She was seen yesterday. And the day before that.”

“Yesterday is not a death.”

“No. It was one of its better qualities.”

A few courtiers laughed. The king did not. He had commissioned a throne with a cupboard beneath it for proclamations, and he was eager to fill it.

The Chancellor pointed to an arrow. “This one left.”

“For another kingdom. We watched her until she went.”

“And this square?”

“He was taken alive and killed by royal order, for research. The date is in the other register.”

“Then give him a bell.”

“A different kind. When would he have died without the order?”

The Chancellor frowned at the square. “How should I know?”

“Exactly.”

He jabbed at a dot. “And this one?”

“Still here when I closed the book for your inquiry.” She turned the page toward him. “Like my mother.”

3. The three strips

The archivist took three strips of paper from her sleeve, each ruled in years.

“One subject watched for two years, then a death.” She set a little bell at the end of the first strip. “Another watched for one year, then transferred. A third watched for three years, still here when the book closed.” She marked the ends with an arrow and a dot.

“Three subjects,” said the king.

“Six years watched, and one death bell.”

The Chancellor pushed the second strip aside. “What if the traveler died on the road?”

“Then someone else may have heard the bell. We did not.” She pushed it back. “But we did see her before she left.”

Behind the archivist stood shelves of paper strips, cut at birthdays and sorted by their owners’ ages. On each shelf, a cord showed their combined length, with the bells beside it.

The Chancellor pointed. “More bells on this shelf. The older subjects must be in greater danger.”

The archivist lifted its cord. It uncoiled almost to the door. The cord on the younger shelf reached only as far as the king’s foot.

“We watched much longer at these ages,” she said. “There was more time for bells to sound.”

The king drew his foot back as though the cord might bite.

4. The missing households

Lady Vetch, who approved transfers, lifted a strip by its arrow.

“Were the ones we sent away ill already?” she asked. “Or did the other kingdoms ask for our strongest?”

“The book doesn’t tell me,” said the archivist.

“Then why use their lines?” asked the Chancellor.

“We saw them while they were here. But Lady Vetch is right to ask who left. If they were different from those who stayed, the answer may mislead us.”

“Some were sent to fill empty households,” Lady Vetch said. “Others were requested by name. I can bring you the orders.”

“Please do.”

The king tapped his throne. “I asked for a verdict. You shall give it before the next bell, or the infirmary doors will be barred.”

The archivist thought of her mother waiting for an attendant to lift a cup to her mouth. She could tell the king that the blank space after every line was a promise. Perhaps he would tear up the notice.

Then someone else would need a cup held, or a joint eased, and the king would say there was no reason to be ill in a kingdom that could not die.

She turned to the shelves.

5. The ragged shelf

The archivist showed the king the shelves from the youngest adults to the oldest.

“For the time we watched, the bells show no clear climb,” she said.

“So they do not become more frequent,” said the king.

“In this kingdom’s book, from six months onward, I couldn’t find a clear climb. That is what I can say.” She reached for the upper shelf.

Only a few strips lay there, their ends curled from handling. She spread them with her fingertips. There was so much bare wood between them that she could see the grain.

“With so little watching at the oldest ages, a rise might be hiding from us.”

She handed him the written verdict, its torn edge left untouched. The king read the first sentence and returned it without looking at the rest. He signed the order to close the infirmary and handed it to the Chancellor.

“Prepare a proclamation. My subjects are immortal.”

“Shall I put that under your name, Majesty?”

“Whose else?”

Down the passage, someone began hammering a hasp onto the infirmary door. The blows reached the chamber one at a time. The archivist counted four before she realized she was counting.

6. The musicians bell

Before the proclamation could be read, the court musician died.

He had come to rehearse for the king’s address. He was old enough to have played at the archivist’s naming, and he ended every ceremony with the same tune, adding a flourish whenever a speech went on too long. He had promised her mother a visit that afternoon, with the instrument that had the cracked ivory key.

Now it lay across his lap. The melody on the sheet before him stopped halfway down the page.

The chamber fell silent. The archivist went to him and closed the lid of his instrument. It made a small, ordinary click. She folded the unfinished music and put it in her pocket; her mother would ask what he had brought.

“One bell,” the king said.

“Yes.”

“Does it mean you were wrong?”

“One bell cannot settle what happens across all the shelves. Nor did the shelves say there would be no more bells.”

She asked a page for a bag, ten wooden figures from the royal game cupboard, and ten tokens.

The Chancellor frowned. “Is this a game?”

“It is today.”

7. The bag of figures

Nine tokens were plain; one bore a black spot. The archivist put them in the bag and shook it. For each figure on the bench she drew a token, showed it to the court, and returned it before drawing for the next. When the spotted token appeared, she took a figure away.

On the first round she removed one. On the second she removed two. On the third she removed none.

The king reached for the bag. “You put the spot back each time?”

“Each time. You may count.”

He did. Nine plain tokens, one spotted. She continued, drawing once for every figure still standing. By the fifth round, four remained.

She put all ten back and began again. This time the spotted token appeared first for the figure at the far end; in the earlier game that figure had lasted until the fourth round. After five rounds, six figures remained.

“The same bag,” Lady Vetch said.

“It tells us no subject’s chance,” said the archivist. “It’s only a game.”

The king looked at the musician’s empty chair.

The bag stayed the same. The benches did not.

8. Room to continue

The king held out his hand for the closure order. The Chancellor gave it to him, carefully turning the royal signature uppermost.

For a moment the king studied it. Then he looked at the cupboard beneath his throne.

“Keep the infirmary open,” he said.

He tore the order in half. He tore it again, with some difficulty, because the royal seal was thick.

“And bring Lady Vetch’s transfer orders to the archivist.”

“What shall we do with the lines that have no bell?” asked the Chancellor.

“Keep them too. With their arrows and squares and dots.”

The hammering stopped.

The archivist returned to the infirmary. Her mother had persuaded the attendant to move her chair near the warm wall and was complaining that the soup needed salt. The archivist gave her the folded music and sat with her until the cup was empty.

Later, at her desk, she opened the book. She found the musician’s entry and drew its bell with a steady hand. Then she found her mother’s name and took up the ruler.

Her mother’s line ran one day farther across the page; beside it, the musician’s ended at a small bell.

Afterword

The kingdom represents a captive colony of naked mole-rats; the ledger represents observations beginning at six months. Time observed before transfer, research euthanasia, or the end of data collection contributes to estimates without supplying a natural age at death. The researchers reported no detectable increase in mortality with age under the observed conditions, but the oldest observations were sparse, and estimates can be affected by why observation ends. Ruby, Smith and Buffenstein, GeroScience (2024).

The eLife assessment of the related reviewed preprint judged evidence for the broader claim of no actuarial senescence incomplete at younger ages and inadequate at older ages. The court, infirmary, musician and token game are inventions; the game illustrates a steady positive chance, not a measured individual probability.

Separately, Horvath and colleagues, Nature Aging found age-associated DNA methylation changes in naked mole-rat tissues. Their relationship to function and mortality remains unresolved. The story does not use coat marks as a counterpart.
