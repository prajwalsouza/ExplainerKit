Why Matching Colours Can Look Different in Another Light

Explainer · 7 minute read. This is the complete recorded narration, with paragraph breaks preserved.

This is a script to read. References to pictures, demonstrations and sounds describe the planned production.

1. First match

Here are two fibres under the first lamp. Same background, side by side. Do they look like the same colour to you?

If you were examining these in a crime lab and decided they matched, would you expect them to contain the same dyes? I would have been tempted to say yes: if the colour is the same, surely whatever made the colour is the same too.

There’s a second lamp waiting beside them, and I want to know what you think will happen when it comes on: will the match survive under that light?

2. Courtroom match

In Atlanta in 1981, a crime-lab microanalyst named Larry Peterson described taking a violet fibre from Nathaniel Cater’s head with tweezers.

Peterson compared it with a fibre from Wayne Williams’s bedspread and reported no significant microscopic differences between them. He had made a comparison, and that observation was something a court could consider alongside the rest of the evidence.

But a similarity is not a source. Other fibres can share characteristics, and a fibre’s appearance can change after it leaves its source. I find that distinction surprisingly easy to lose: a match feels like an answer, even when it is the beginning of a much larger question.

3. Ribbon records

Long before Peterson looked at those fibres, Thomas Sutton helped James Clerk Maxwell make three photographs of a tartan ribbon in 1861: one through a red filter, one through green, and one through blue.

Maxwell projected the records through corresponding filters and lined up the images. A coloured ribbon appeared on the screen. I love the economy of it: three separate pictures, each with a limited view, giving people a colour impression without telling them everything the ribbon was made of.

It wasn’t a practical colour-photography process, but it left a question hanging in the air: how much can a small set of records tell us about what produced it?

4. Two lamps

Back to our lamps. Both look white, but white light is a mixture, and wavelength is one way of describing the different parts of that light, from shorter wavelengths to longer ones.

The two curves show how much light each lamp sends out at each wavelength. Here, one curve is higher: that lamp supplies more light at this wavelength. Farther along, the other lamp supplies more.

That difference matters because the fibres cannot reflect light the lamp hasn’t supplied; change the balance of light arriving at them, and you give their different responses a different mixture to work on.

5. Fibre filter

Follow the light into a dyed fibre. Some is absorbed, some passes through, and some is reflected toward our eyes; the proportions depend on the wavelength.

I think of that behaviour as the fibre’s filter, though there’s no little sheet tucked inside the thread. Imagine two bands of light reaching two fibres: in this simplified example, the first fibre reflects more of the short-wavelength band, while the second reflects more of the long-wavelength band.

That’s only two bands. A real comparison involves the light and the fibres’ responses across a whole range of wavelengths, so the mixture reaching your eye depends on both the lamp and the fibre.

Change the incoming mixture, and the outgoing mixture can change too. Now the light has reached the eye; what happens to all that information?

6. Three buckets

In ordinary daylight-level colour vision, the eye has three classes of cone cells, each sensitive to a range of wavelengths. Those ranges overlap, so the same part of the light contributes to more than one class’s response.

Here’s my favourite picture for that: three buckets, each collecting contributions from the whole mixture in its own way. For one bucket, imagine one mixture contributes two units from one part of the spectrum and four from another; a different mixture contributes three and three. Both fill that bucket to six.

That arithmetic is a toy example, but the idea matters: different mixtures can produce the same levels across all three buckets, because each cone class pools contributions instead of giving the brain a wavelength-by-wavelength receipt.

So a colour match doesn’t tell you the entire spectrum. I didn’t expect such a small summary to leave so much room for different answers.

7. Overlapping shadows

Imagine two coins standing in a line. From one end of the table, the nearer coin hides the outline of the other, and you see a single round shape.

Move sideways and the separation appears. The first view was real, but it left two objects tangled together in one outline; the second view reveals something the first did not.

8. The match diverges

One last prediction: when the second lamp comes on, does the match survive?

Under the first lamp, these two fibres look alike. Now the second lamp comes on—and for this prepared pair, the match separates. One comparison, two different results.

Here’s the model that makes sense of it. Different spectra entering the eye from the two fibres can fill the three buckets to the same levels, so a match under the first lamp does not require those spectra to be identical. A match made by different spectra is called a metameric match.

The second lamp changes the balance of wavelengths falling on both fibres. In our model, their different responses to that light produce different bucket levels, and the colours separate; the observed match alone does not measure those levels or prove exactly what their spectra are.

I wouldn’t bet on any lamp pulled from a cupboard doing this. The pair and the light have to do the work.

Now try a different case: two paint samples match under one lamp. Does that prove they contain the same pigments, or that they’ll look different under every other lamp? Neither conclusion follows from the first match. The match is useful information, but it leaves both questions open.

9. Testing fibres

There is another way to ask what those fibres are doing with light. A forensic examiner can use a microspectrophotometer: a microscope joined to an instrument that measures a tiny sample’s optical response across wavelengths.

Instead of recording only that a fibre looks blue, the examiner gets a curve to compare with another curve. That gives the examiner more information than a colour judgment alone, although a curve does not automatically name the dye that made it.

There’s a chemical question too: what components are in the colouring material? With a suitable sample and method, an examiner can extract dyes and use chromatography to separate components for further analysis. Extraction alters or consumes evidence, which matters when a tiny piece of fibre is all you have.

10. Engs blue fibres

In 2009, Mary Eng and her colleagues studied blue textile fibres made with different colourants or different proportions of them, even though the fibres looked alike.

The team measured the fibres with ultraviolet-visible microspectrophotometry, from about 350 to 800 nanometres. That range includes ultraviolet as well as visible light, and the measurements distinguished the samples’ spectra despite their visual similarity.

I like that result because it gives us something firmer than a dramatic verdict: fibres that looked alike in the study yielded distinguishable measurements, while the curves did not automatically tell the researchers where a fibre came from.

The team also tested a proposed rule that the curves of metameric samples must cross at least three times. A crossing is a wavelength where two measured curves have the same value; some of the tested samples’ curves crossed, but others did not cross at all.

11. Same colour

The lamp leaves me with a question. If the fibres match under one light and differ under another, what do we mean when we say they’re the same colour?

One answer begins with the surfaces. A reflectance profile describes how much light a surface reflects at different wavelengths; philosophers Alex Byrne and David Hilbert defend an account in which colours are types of surface reflectance. On their view, two different profiles can still belong to the same colour type.

Jonathan Cohen takes a relational approach, understanding colour in relation to perceivers and circumstances. Describing our pair from that direction brings the observer and the light into the account.

The observed change under the lamps is one question; what colour is, philosophically, is another. I like that the same pair of fibres can make the first question clear and still leave us arguing carefully about the second.

12. Return to the lamp

Back under the first lamp, the two fibres look alike again for this observer under these conditions. That first observation hasn’t become false just because a second light gave us another one.

I keep hearing the courtroom phrase: a similarity, not a source. Another light can expose a difference, and optical or chemical tests add information; neither a colour match nor a changed appearance tells us by itself where a fibre came from, especially when exposure can change how a fibre looks.

Under this light, they still match—and now we know how much that leaves unseen.
