Where Did the Missing Numbers Go?

1. Bell at dawn

At dawn, Mara’s mother stood beneath the prosperity bell with a porter’s hook over one shoulder. The hook had a red wool tassel tied to its handle, so she could find it among the other hooks when the porters stopped for tea.

Mara climbed the board’s little ladder and wrote the morning reading: 1,000.000.

Her mother tapped the bell. It answered with a thin, empty note.

“Good luck,” she told it.

“There’s nothing in there,” Mara said.

“That’s why it needs the luck.”

Her mother caught the tassel before it brushed her face and went to work. Mara watched her take the first bale from a cart, bend under it, and disappear between the shutters.

2. The narrow slate

Mara worked in a booth beside the bell, where the window stuck in wet weather and the chalk tasted of salt. A trader pushed a notice through the hatch: oil had gone up at one of the stalls named in the council ledger.

Mara opened the ledger to its rule and worked out the new reading. Her paper said 1,000.0006. Her slate had only three compartments after the dot, so she copied 1,000.000 into them and cut the 6 from the paper with a small knife.

She erased the old reading and took the slate to the board. When a wool merchant brought the next notice, she began with the reading on the slate. This time her answer was 999.875. It fitted, and the knife stayed on the table.

The ledger’s instructions were for readings above zero. Mara signed each page with a neat tail on the final a. The councillors admired her columns. Three figures after every dot, all in a line: they said you could see at once that the city’s affairs were in good hands.

She kept the cuttings in a glass jar. When anyone asked why, she shook it and said, “My wages. The council pays me in crumbs.”

The joke improved as the jar filled.

3. A market of scraps

In the months that followed, prices went both ways. A poor oil harvest sent one price up; a ship with a split sail arrived late and sent another down. The bell’s reading, through all the changes, sank.

At first the merchants complained of the porters’ tea breaks. Then they complained of their wages. At a council meeting, one merchant pointed at the reading on the board.

“With the bell so low,” he said, “we can’t afford so many hooks.”

Mara’s mother came home with a notice folded into her apron. Unless the reading recovered, her contract would end at the close of the month.

They rented two rooms above the cooper’s yard. In the back room, rain came through a crack beside the chimney and fell into a bowl. Her mother put the notice beneath the bowl to flatten it.

“I can find work at the south market,” she said. “They still carry things there.”

“And a room?”

Her mother took the notice from under the bowl. The bottom edge was wet.

That night Mara emptied the jar on the table. Hundreds of pale strips spilled across the wood. She sorted them by the pages from which she had cut them, wrote down what each discarded part was worth, and began adding them. If she could show the council the missing amount, perhaps she could put it back.

She worked until the cooper below stopped hammering. The sum was small. She stared at it, then at the notice, and checked her addition from the beginning.

4. Three small additions

The next evening Mara brought home two slates and set them between the bread and the bowl. “This isn’t the market’s rule,” she said. “It’s something simpler. Watch.”

On the narrow slate she wrote 10.000. She added 0.0006 on paper, making 10.0006, and copied only 10.000 onto the slate. She used that 10.000 for the next addition. Again she obtained 10.0006 and copied 10.000. She added 0.0006 a third time to that same shortened reading, and again the slate said 10.000.

On the wider slate she began at 10.000 and kept the whole result each time. The first addition gave 10.0006. The second, starting there, gave 10.0012. The third gave 10.0018.

Her mother leaned over the table. “You put three things in. Why has nothing arrived?”

Mara pointed to the eraser.

Her mother examined the narrow slate. “I should like one of those for my rent.”

Mara laughed, then stopped. She wrapped the wider slate in a cloth and went to ask the archivist for the price notices.

5. The wider record

The price notices were kept in the council archive, tied in bundles with green cord. The archivist brought them out on a trolley whose left wheel squealed. Mara checked that the bundles ran from the first reading to the most recent change, then carried them, one at a time, to the long table.

She began again at 1,000.000. Beside the old ledger she worked through the notices in their original order, following the market’s rule. This time she used a wide slate and kept the extra figures for the next calculation. When the figures threatened to run off its edge, she added another slate. She checked her work against the notices and began sections again whenever she found a copying mistake.

The reading she reached was much higher than the one beneath the bell.

She compared the difference with the sum from her jar. They did not match. She checked the two sums again, then crossed out the jar total she had written beside the gap.

For the public board she copied only three figures after the dot from her wider result. Underneath, on the working slate, the remaining figures stayed where they were. When she tried the next notice, she began with that fuller reading.

The archivist watched her wrap the slates in cloth. “Will they listen?” he asked.

“They listened when I wrote the other one.”

“That is not what I asked.”

6. The last contract

The council heard her on the last afternoon before the contracts were due. Mara laid the slates on its table, with the price notices beside them.

The councillor for trade looked at the wider reading. “This is an unofficial invention.”

“It follows the same notices,” Mara said. “I kept more of each answer.”

The councillor opened the old ledger. Mara’s signature stood at the foot of every page. He turned several leaves, letting each settle before turning the next.

“You signed these.”

“Yes.”

“And now you say they are wrong?”

“I wrote what I was told to write.” Her voice came out smaller than she intended. “I used what was left for the next change. I can show you.”

The councillor laid his hand over one of her signatures. Her mother stood at the back of the chamber, packing her hook into its canvas sleeve. The red tassel caught in the buckle. She pulled it free without looking up.

Mara remembered the wet edge of the notice beneath the bowl.

Her mother started toward the door. Mara followed her as far as the threshold. Then she turned back, untied the cloth from the wider slates and placed them in front of the councillors, alongside the old ledger and the notices.

“Please go through them with me,” she said.

They argued until the chamber lamps were lit. At last they agreed to inspect the work and decide in the morning. The market would be closed for the rest day; no new notices would come in.

7. The closed market

At dawn the councillors came out beneath the bell. Mara had been awake most of the night with them and had chalk on both sleeves. One councillor held the corrected reading on a board, covered with a cloth.

He pulled the cloth away.

The number was much higher. A man in the crowd cheered. The councillor for trade, who had examined the slates until his eyes watered, called for a feast.

Mara’s mother looked down the street. Every stall was shuttered. A dog slept across the fishmonger’s step, and the tea seller’s kettle was cold.

“Who’s paying for it?” she asked.

The cheer stopped. Someone laughed.

Mara set the notices and the two records on the table beneath the bell. With one finger she traced the same sequence in both: the old reading shortened at every step, the wider one carried forward with its extra figures.

“Same notices,” she said. “A different reading.”

The council withdrew the notice and restored her mother’s contract. It also began an argument about whether the bell ought to decide a porter’s work at all. The argument was still going when Mara put the slates away.

8. Other compartments

Later that morning a clerk from a neighboring city came to see the slates. She took a small box from her pocket and opened it. Inside were rows of compartments, each holding a dark bead or none at all. She tried to arrange the beads to match a quantity Mara had written, frowned, and rearranged them twice.

“Near enough for our board,” she said, closing the lid. “Our rules tell us which arrangement to choose.”

“May I see them?” Mara asked.

“If you visit. Bring your own eraser. Ours have a habit of disappearing.”

At the porter’s cart, Mara’s mother stood beneath the bell with her restored contract in one hand and her hook in the other. She had already asked about the south market. There might be work there, the carter had told her, though he could promise nothing.

She folded the contract and tucked it into her apron. Then she tapped the bell, listened to its hollow note, and hung her hook on the cart.

Afterword

The bell stands for the Vancouver Stock Exchange index, an indicator of selected share prices, not a pot of investors’ money. Contemporary reports describe digits being repeatedly cut and the resulting accumulated error; they report a corrected opening figure of 1,098.892 in November 1983. See the contemporary reports. A later numerical account explains the reuse of the shortened value. The original code and complete reconstruction were not available for independent verification.

Mara’s addition test is invented; it is not the historical formula. For positive values, cutting after three decimal places lowers a value or leaves it unchanged. Cutting toward zero moves a negative value upward. Later changes can affect how earlier differences propagate.

The slates suggest the limits of storing numbers in computers, but actual formats and rounding rules differ. The neighboring clerk’s beads gesture toward binary floating point: in common formats, decimal 0.1 is stored as a nearby binary fraction, not exactly. That is separate from Vancouver’s reported decimal cutting. See the Python floating-point tutorial. Displaying fewer digits need not change the fuller stored value used in later calculations.
