What Happens to a Sound Along the Way?

Series · 15 minute read. This is the complete recorded narration, with paragraph breaks preserved.

On this pageHearing patternsPattern in electricityShared sample pointsSound against soundRecorded sources

Back to the examples · Follow the process

The writing and source links are model outputs from a recorded authoring run. The claims have not been independently rechecked for this reading page.

This is one of two treatments of the same sound unit. The series process combined the objectives into a single piece. Bracketed playback directions describe planned demonstrations.

Hearing patterns

1. Hear the mystery

Listen to this note. [Play 200 Hz reference.] Now this one. [Play mixture.] Do you hear a low pitch in both? The second sound has a different character, and that low pitch won’t be clear to everyone, but I keep reaching for the same note underneath both sounds. Here’s the strange part: when we look at what went into the second sound, the answer is not what I expected. For now, its ingredients stay hidden. All we have is this wavering trace and a question: what is it about that shape that reaches your ear?

2. Count the waves

That trace shows what the air is doing at one spot beside your ear: pressure rises above its usual level, falls below it, and rises again as a sound passes. When a pattern repeats, we can count how many complete repeats pass that spot in one second. That count is frequency. One repeat per second is one hertz. Here’s 400 hertz, then 600. [Play both.] The second generally sounds higher; that sense of height is pitch. Frequency is a physical count, while pitch is what a listener experiences.

3. Seebecks siren

A German physicist named August Seebeck was puzzling over a related question in the 1840s, during a dispute with Georg Simon Ohm about how musical tones should be understood. Seebeck turned to a siren and listened to the puffs of air made by its rotating, perforated discs. Not a police siren. In one experiment with three discs, he reported a strong low pitch where the corresponding low-frequency component appeared to be absent. His apparatus was different from our electronic mixture, but his report leaves us with a question I want answered.

4. What do we hear

Was that low note an ingredient in the sound, or was it something Seebeck heard in the pattern made by the other ingredients? I like that question because it gives us two things to look for as we build a sound of our own.

5. Stack the waves

Here’s our wave-stacker. Its three ingredients are a 400 hertz sine wave, a 600 hertz sine wave, and an 800 hertz sine wave. [Play each.] Now they play together. At every instant, their pressure changes add: if two are pushing the pressure upward while the third is pulling it down, the final height depends on all three contributions. The result is that wavering trace from the opening. It looks a little untidy, which I find reassuring. But does that shape ever repeat exactly?

6. The five millisecond shape

It does repeat. In five milliseconds—that’s five thousandths of a second—the 400 hertz wave completes two cycles, the 600 completes three, and the 800 completes four, so all three return to the same positions they had at the start. Their combined shape starts again. Five milliseconds per repeat means 200 repeats in one second. Now look at the spectrum: lines at 400, 600 and 800 hertz, but no 200 hertz sine-wave component. There’s our surprise. [Play 200 Hz reference, then mixture.] The whole shape repeats at 200 hertz, even though a 200 hertz sine wave is not one of its ingredients.

7. What the listener hears

I expected that low note to be sitting in the mixture as a separate tone. It isn’t. Yet this combination can give a listener a pitch near the 200 hertz reference, even though its three components are all higher. If you didn’t hear a clear low note, that’s a result too; the sound and the way it reaches you matter. The ear and brain make use of relationships in the sound, though exactly how the nervous system combines information about frequency and timing to produce pitch is still being studied.

8. Pattern and perception

The low note you hear isn’t one of the three tones we put in. It’s something the pattern can bring out for a listener. The pressure trace shows how the air changes at one place, and the spectrum shows the components we used to make that same signal; neither drawing is a picture of what hearing it feels like. [Replay reference, then mixture.] I keep coming back to that untidy shape: a physical pattern repeats, and a listener hears a pitch.

9. Next chapter transition

A pressure pattern has reached the ear. Imagine another arriving at the same place, timed so that whenever the first pushes upward, the second pulls downward by the same amount. Their pressure changes add to zero there. The original source hasn’t stopped; it’s the combined pressure at that spot that changes. I’m left with another question: how could a pattern like this be carried into a different physical form?

10. Bridge

We’ve been following a pattern in air, all the way to the ear. But the ear isn’t the only thing that can move in response to it. What if we let that changing pressure move a membrane and follow what happens next?

Pattern in electricity

11. Voice and line

Someone you know says your name from another room, and before you see them, you know who it is. On the screen, a line wavers as they speak: a rise, a dip, a pause, another flurry of movement. What is that line keeping? I want to follow a voice from moving air into a mark, and then into electricity, to see what survives when the air goes still.

12. Scotts line

In the nineteenth century, Édouard-Léon Scott de Martinville wanted to make sound visible, to leave a mark that someone could study after a voice had faded. His phonautograph gathered sound and directed it toward a membrane. As the membrane moved, an attached stylus scratched a wavering line through soot on paper wrapped around a turning cylinder. On April ninth, 1860, the machine traced someone singing “Au Clair de la Lune.” Scott could look at the line, but his machine couldn’t play it back. The singer was gone. The line remained—but could anyone hear a voice in it?

13. Voice reconstructed

Almost a century and a half later, researchers scanned Scott’s surviving trace and used software to follow the shape of the line. Listen. That rough, wavering voice is a reconstruction of the 1860 song, first played in recognizable form in 2008, after the marks had been turned into an audio signal. Later, researchers investigated and corrected the uneven speed of the recording. I find it astonishing that a mark in soot gave them enough to work with, though we still don’t know for certain who sang. The sound wasn’t trapped in the paper; the researchers made it from the trace.

14. Air to membrane

A moving-coil microphone starts with something Scott would recognize: changing air pressure moves a thin surface called a diaphragm back and forth, much as sound moved his membrane. Here they are side by side. Scott’s membrane moved a stylus and made a soot line. What could we attach to the microphone’s moving diaphragm to get an electrical signal instead?

15. Coil game

A magnet moves into a coil of wire connected to a sensitive meter, and the needle swings. What happens when the magnet stops? The needle settles back; when the magnet moves out, it swings the other way. I had to get used to this: having the magnet there isn’t enough to keep the voltage going, because the response comes from the changing magnetic field through the coil. Moving the coil in a magnetic field works too. And we already have something that moves: the diaphragm.

16. Voltage reveal

Inside the microphone, a small coil of wire is attached to the diaphragm and sits in the field of a permanent magnet. As sound moves the diaphragm, the coil moves with it, and that motion induces a changing voltage across the coil. There’s our new line. The microphone shapes the signal, so it isn’t a perfect copy of the air pressure or a direct drawing of the diaphragm’s position. Scott’s stylus left a line in soot. The microphone makes a changing voltage instead. Both respond to a voice, each in its own way. The pressure trace we started with has become motion in a diaphragm and then a changing voltage: the pattern is now represented in a different physical form.

17. Voice back in air

Now listen to this. I speak into a dynamic microphone, pause, and say the same thing again; the line on the screen changes as I speak, then settles during the pause. The microphone is making its signal as I talk, while separate recording equipment saves a representation of it for later. When that recording goes to a loudspeaker, the speaker moves air again—and there’s my voice, back in the room.

18. Line continues

Think back to that person saying your name from the other room: their voice reached you through moving air, and you knew who it was. A mark or a changing voltage can carry information about that voice somewhere else, even though neither preserves the whole event or guarantees you’ll recognize it. I like that Scott’s quiet line and the microphone’s restless voltage are so different to look at, yet both give us something of a voice to work with.

19. Sampling handoff

There’s another question waiting in that electrical line: what happens if, instead of keeping its continuous changes, a recorder takes measurements at separate instants and saves those values?

20. Bridge

The line now represents a changing voltage. Suppose we look at it only at regular instants and keep the values we find. Would those points always tell us which pattern made them?

Shared sample points

21. Falling note

Listen to this note. [Play the falling output.] It slides down, doesn’t it? Now here’s the part I had to hear twice: the tone going into the sampling demonstration was climbing the whole time. The source never turned around. So where did that falling note come from?

22. Two traces

A simple tone reaches your ear as a repeating change in air pressure, and the number of cycles each second is its frequency, measured in hertz. Faster repetition generally sounds higher, though the frequency is a physical rate and the pitch is what you hear. A microphone turns that changing pressure into a changing electrical signal. What happens when a recorder keeps measurements of that signal only at separate ticks of time?

23. Oppenheim sweep

At MIT, signal-processing professor Alan Oppenheim and his colleagues put an input signal on the upper trace of an oscilloscope and the reconstructed output on the lower trace. As they raised the input frequency, the output rose too—until the lower trace turned around. In the sound demonstration, you hear the same thing: the output climbs, then falls, while the input keeps climbing. That’s the sound we heard at the start. I think the two traces look like they’re arguing, and I want to know which bit of the equipment started it.

24. Wagon glimpses

An old western gives us a clue. A wagon rolls forward, but a wheel appears to slow down and turn backward; I’ve spent an unreasonable amount of time staring at wheels because of this. Imagine a white mark on the rim: if the wheel makes nearly a full forward turn between frames, the mark lands just behind its previous position. Frame by frame, that looks like a small backward turn. Both movements fit the pictures, but the frames alone don’t show what happened in the gaps.

25. Dots on voltage

A sampler faces a similar problem. It measures the microphone’s electrical signal at regularly spaced instants, and each measurement becomes a dot on our screen. Once the original curve disappears, a different smooth curve can pass through all the same dots, taking a completely different route through the spaces between them. The dots are real. They tell us the values at those instants, but they don’t directly show the path in between.

26. Predict the dots

Here are two actual possibilities: tones at 1,000 and 7,000 hertz, drawn as waves with the same amplitude and both starting at the top. We measure each one 8,000 times a second. Between measurements, the 1,000-hertz wave moves through one eighth of a cycle, while the 7,000-hertz wave races through seven eighths—nearly a whole turn. There’s our wheel mark again. When the next measurement arrives, will the fast wave be at a different height from the slow one? [Pause.]

27. Reveal shared points

There. Every dot lands on both curves, even though the 7,000-hertz wave and the 1,000-hertz wave take very different routes between measurements. [Play the reconstructed tone.] That’s 1,000 hertz coming out, with 7,000 going in. The machine kept every dot—and still lost which sound made them.

At the next sample, the fast wave is one eighth of a turn short of coming back to the top. The slow wave is one eighth of a turn past it. A cosine has the same height on both sides, so at every tick these two waves meet. Reconstruct those samples as the lower tone and you hear what’s called an alias. Earlier, a missing fundamental complicated the pitch we heard in a complex sound; here, the puzzle is different: two patterns can share the same sample values.

28. Filter before

Here’s the awkward question: once we have those dots, can we clean the mistake out afterward? A digital filter sees the same sequence whether the input was our 7,000-hertz tone or our 1,000-hertz tone, so removing that sequence removes it for both explanations. There’s no label on a dot saying, ‘I came from the fast one.’

The protection has to come before this sampling step. At 8,000 samples a second, half the rate is 4,000 hertz. Before sampling, a low-pass filter weakens unwanted frequencies that could fold into the range we want, including our 7,000-hertz input. Real filters need room to roll off, so they reduce aliases rather than guaranteeing a perfect cutoff. They change the evidence while the two signals are still distinguishable.

29. Return to the ear

Listen to that falling note once more. [Play the output.] I find it a little unsettling that the sound is right there to hear, while the rising input that produced it tells such a different story. The samples were real—but on their own, they couldn’t tell you which sound had made them.

30. Next pattern

There’s another way to change the sound reaching your ear, and this one surprised me for a different reason: adding a second sound can make less sound at one spot. When one pressure variation rises and another falls by the same amount, their sum there shrinks. What would it take to arrange that at your ear?

31. Bridge

Those shared points showed us how two patterns can be hard to tell apart after sampling. Now let’s return to the ear, where two sound patterns can meet in the air. What happens if we arrange for the second one to oppose the first?

Sound against sound

32. Fan switch

The fan is still running. So why does it seem quieter when you switch on noise cancellation? The headphones are making another sound, and somehow that helps. If you have a pair nearby, you can try the comparison with no music playing: keep them on and switch cancellation off, then on. Even switched off, the headphones block some sound; switching cancellation on adds something else. When cancellation reduces the hum, the pressure swings associated with it become smaller. How does adding sound do that?

33. One listener

The fan hasn’t received a polite request to stop. It keeps pushing on the air, but what reaches your ear changes when another source adds its own pressure variations at the same place. That struck me as strange at first: the fan is doing the same job as before, yet the sound reaching one listener can be different. So what would that second source need to do?

34. Orchestra for one

Imagine an orchestra playing in a hall, and give a second orchestra an outrageously difficult assignment: make the sound smaller at one listener’s seat. Not the whole hall. Just that seat, where the pressure changes from both orchestras meet and add together, moment by moment, as the music goes on. I would like to see that rehearsal. Let’s start with something easier than an orchestra: two simple waves.

35. Shift the second wave

First, the two waves have equal swings, and their peaks arrive together at the listener’s seat. Add them, and the peaks are twice as high and the troughs twice as deep. Our helpful orchestra has made things worse. Now shift the second wave by half a cycle, so that wherever the first is one unit above the usual air pressure, the second is one unit below it. Plus one and minus one add to zero. As the waves continue in that perfectly matched relationship, their combined trace stays flat. That’s the bit I love: adding another sound can make the pressure swings smaller.

36. The seat matters

There’s a catch. If our listener moves to another seat, the sounds from the two orchestras travel along different paths, and their peaks and troughs no longer have to meet in the same relationship. A flat trace at the first seat doesn’t promise one at the second. We were already asking two orchestras to coordinate for one person, so I’m ready to abandon the concert-hall plan. Bring the second sound close to the listener instead, and aim for the small space around an ear.

37. Opposing trace reveal

Here’s the fan trace again, arriving at the ear, and here comes a second trace from the headphone speaker. Where one goes up, the other goes down; add their pressure changes point by point, and in this illustration the combined trace shrinks. That is the payoff. A microphone senses sound outside the earcup, inside it, or both, and a controller uses that information to guide the speaker. Getting the match right means accounting for the time and paths the sounds travel, because flipping a microphone signal alone doesn’t guarantee the right pressure at the ear. Real headphones leave some sound behind. Our opening pressure trace has returned, and beside it an opposing trace shows how their sum can have smaller variations near the ear.

38. Measured quiet

How much difference does it make outside our drawing? In one study, 47 people listened to simulated bus noise through four models of wireless earbuds, and switching cancellation on reduced measured low-frequency sound by roughly 20 to 29 decibels on average, depending on the model. That’s a result for those earbuds and that test, not a promise for every sound. I like having the number: the little drawing at the ear is describing something people can actually measure.

39. Return to the ear

What reaches your ear depends on how sounds meet there: pressure from the fan and pressure from the speaker add together, and the result depends on their size and timing. The fan is still turning. Here, the two traces join, and the line at the listener’s ear becomes smaller. Another sound has helped make the hum quieter.

40. Closing

There’s our wavering trace again, at the listener’s ear. An opposing trace joins it, and their sum becomes smaller there. Along the way, we’ve seen a pattern move between physical forms, learned what separate measurements can leave uncertain, and heard how another sound can change the result. What we hear depends both on what a signal contains and on how sounds combine.
