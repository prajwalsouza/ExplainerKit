# What Are You Filtering Out?

## 1. The unwanted hum

There’s a voice here, and another sound underneath it. [Play original; let the speaker stop.] That little pause lets us hear the hum on its own, even though the microphone caught it together with the voice.

Here’s the same passage after some processing. [Play filtered version.] I find myself leaning toward the voice in the second version, and I’m tempted to call the job finished: the hum was in the way, and now there’s less of it.

But what if the sound we just reduced is evidence somebody else needs?

## 2. Listen again

Here’s a kitchen recording: somebody talking, with a clock ticking nearby. [Play clip.] Now the very same recording, but this time the question is how many ticks fit into ten seconds. [Replay; pause.]

The clock hasn’t changed its mind about being a clock; we’ve changed what we came to hear, and suddenly the voice that mattered a moment ago is competing with the ticks.

Neither job is foolish. If the sentence matters, the ticks are a nuisance; if we’re checking the clock’s rate, the ticks are the point. Noise for which question?

## 3. Ribbon and notch

Here’s a steady tone. [Play tone.] Its vibration repeats a certain number of times each second; that rate is its frequency, measured in hertz.

As it plays, a stripe appears on this picture. Time runs left to right, and frequency runs from low at the bottom to high at the top. [Raise pitch.] The stripe moves up. [Lower volume.] It dims: brightness shows how strong the sound is at that frequency.

Now I’ve made an unfairly tidy recording: a clean voice with a steady fifty-hertz tone added, meaning the tone repeats fifty cycles every second. [Play mixture, then filtered copy.] A filter called a notch reduces a narrow band around fifty hertz, and the stripe darkens.

There’s no little ear inside the filter deciding which sound is the nuisance. It turns down that frequency band, so voice energy there is affected too, while hum at other frequencies, including harmonics, can remain.

Our generated tone is perfectly steady; it is a way to see the filter’s rule, not a record of the grid changing. I’d keep the mixture as well as the filtered copy, because somebody arriving with another question deserves a chance to hear what we started with.

## 4. The changing line

Cătălin Grigoraș examined recordings offered as evidence, including a recording made by a secret surveillance system. He was investigating whether changes connected to electrical network frequency could help assess when recordings were made and whether they showed signs of alteration.

In much of Europe, the grid’s nominal frequency is fifty hertz, but the actual frequency shifts a little over time. In some recordings, electrical interference or sound from grid-powered equipment carries those changes into the audio.

Our generated tone makes a flat line. A grid-related trace can wander. I expected a clue about time to look like numbers stamped on a file, not a wobble hiding inside a sound.

The line can be faint and crowded by other sounds, and a hum near fifty hertz alone doesn’t establish that it tracks the grid. If an examiner recovers a usable trace, though, there’s somewhere else to look for the same wiggle.

## 5. Find the trace

Finding that line is a problem in its own right. Ode Ojowu and colleagues studied ways to estimate the grid’s slowly changing frequency when other signals interfere, using its gradual movement to help track it through a recording.

In their reported tests, their approach estimated frequency more accurately than the comparison method they used under the conditions they studied. That’s useful progress, though it doesn’t turn every hum into a usable trace.

## 6. Match with limits

The other record is a time-stamped measurement of the relevant grid. Imagine sliding the line recovered from an audio recording across a longer reference: here the bends disagree, but over here they follow one another more closely. That closer fit suggests a time worth investigating.

That little sliding picture is an illustration. Maarten Huijbregtse and Zeno Geradts tested real recordings against two weeks of reference data, and the result depended on both the length of the recording and how they compared the lines.

With one minute, their squared-error method found none of the fourteen correct times. With ten minutes, it found all fourteen. Change the matching method, and the shorter clips improved.

Squared error penalizes numerical differences between the lines; correlation looks at how their patterns move together. These are results from that experiment, not a promise that ten minutes always works or that one minute never does.

A suitable match can support a proposed time. It doesn’t identify the room or the person holding the recorder, and it doesn’t establish that the words spoken were true.

## 7. Stars made quiet

Here’s another kind of comparison. Imagine two drawings of the same patch of night sky: most stars are equally bright in both, but one has changed. Subtract the first drawing from the second, and the equal dots cancel while the changing one stands out.

Henrietta Swan Leavitt did something related at Harvard, comparing photographs of the same sky from different nights to find stars whose brightness changed. Our subtraction is a teaching model, not a reconstruction of her exact procedure.

Now imagine a star outside the frame of both pictures. Neither view includes it, so their agreement cannot bring it back; I like calling that a shared blind spot.

## 8. What survived the soil

A gram of soil from the Peyton Randolph property in Williamsburg, Virginia, offers a different picture of what survives. A researcher looking for pollen spent two working days at a microscope examining that sparse sample and counted 146 grains.

Some grains were well preserved; others were damaged. The report considered several explanations for the low yield, including degradation and the possibility that water carried grains down and out of the coarse soil.

I keep picturing those two days spent looking at one speck at a time, knowing that the sample had already been through a history before it reached the microscope.

## 9. The stripe revealed

Now the dim stripe has a second meaning. The notch reduces a narrow frequency band whether someone wants to hear the words or examine the background, because frequency is the rule it follows.

In a suitable recording, that same band could contain a recoverable grid-related trace. Reducing the hum there can also weaken the evidence an examiner wants, even while making the voice easier for someone else to follow.

We haven’t established a grid trace in our opening recording, and our generated tone was steady. What surprised me is that a useful choice for one listening job could make a different job harder.

## 10. What copies share

Here’s the preserved original, followed by two copies processed in different ways. [Play all three.] The background has changed. Listen to what happened to the voice, too.

There’s another comparison: line up the original and a processed copy in time and level, subtract one from the other, and listen to what changed. [Play difference.] It isn’t a little jar containing only hum; altered voice and effects of processing can be in there too.

Now remember the star outside both frames. Suppose the recorder introduced a sound before we made either copy: all three files could share it, so their agreement alone wouldn’t tell us whether it came from the room or the recorder.

A separate recording made with different equipment could help test that possibility, although we would still need to ask what the two setups share.

## 11. Hear it once more

One more case: a wildlife recording is filtered to make a bird call clearer, and later another researcher wants to study insects in the background. Which file does each need, and what could both files still miss? [Pause.]

The filtered copy may help the bird researcher; the preserved original gives the insect researcher the unfiltered recording to examine. Neither file can recover a sound the microphone never captured.

Philosophers James Bogen and James Boyd discuss how observation depends on instruments and assumptions. Think of a thermometer: we use its reading because we have reasons to trust the instrument, and checking it against another thermometer could give us a reason to revise that trust. The question directs our attention, but the evidence can still push back.

Here’s the opening recording once more. [Play original; let the hum continue after the voice stops.] What is the hum obscuring? And what, in a different recording, might it contain? We kept this file so another question could be asked of it.

The hum is still there—and now you can hear why someone might want to keep it.
